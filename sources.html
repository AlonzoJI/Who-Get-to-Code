Methods and sources

How the numbers on this site were gathered and checked

Every figure on this site comes from a named source, with a date and a link. Before a number was marked verified, its wording was checked against the source itself, and the exact sentence was saved in the data files under /data. Anything not yet checked is shown with a placeholder badge.

Showing the copy built from the /data files when the site was last deployed.

How the datasets fit together

Figures are shown as each source reported them. The site does not merge datasets into new statistics, with two exceptions. Some pages show simple arithmetic on published numbers, such as the 15-point gap between 53% and 38%, and the note always says so. The pipeline diagram on the overview page will multiply each stage's rate once those rates are verified; until then it removes no one.

The six pipeline stages draw on different datasets: Code.org and the Civil Rights Data Collection for access and enrollment, College Board for AP exams, IPEDS for college enrollment and degrees, NSF NCSES and the American Community Survey for jobs and earnings.

What the data can't tell you

  • Different groups of students. Each dataset follows its own cohort and year, so the pipeline describes a typical path, not one class of students followed for ten years.
  • Different definitions. Sources count shares in some places and raw enrollment in others, use "students" or "high school students," and draw their own lines around "elite" or "highly selective" colleges. Notes on each figure say which.
  • Secondhand reporting. Several figures come from news coverage of a report rather than the report itself. The figure table says which source was read.
  • Too soon to tell. Most outcome data covers only the first one or two classes after 2023, and the newest federal data lags by a year or more.
A data problem

The end of corporate diversity reports limits what can be tracked

Google, Meta and Microsoft did not publish diversity data in 2025. Their reports were among the few public records of who gets hired in tech and at what level. Without them, stage 06 of the pipeline depends on federal surveys, which don't report company by company and come out more slowly, so changes inside individual companies can't be followed from the outside.

Sources: Allwork.Space, Nov. 7, 2025; Engadget, Nov. 22, 2025

Two kinds of data

Published statistics and community submissions are kept apart

Published statistics

Figures from reports, studies, government data and news coverage. Each one names its source, year and link, and was checked against the source before it was marked verified. These are the numbers in the charts, stat tiles, pipeline and timeline, and the only ones in the figure table below.

Community submissions

Answers that K-12 educators and STEM outreach leaders send through the forms on Share from your classroom and the outreach directory. They describe what people see in their own schools and programs. They are self-reported and not a representative sample: whoever chooses to answer is counted, so they can't stand in for all schools or all programs. They appear only after review, are always labeled as self-reported, and are never mixed into the published statistics.

Privacy

What the forms collect and what anyone can see

Nothing anyone submits appears on the site until the site's author reviews and approves it. Both forms ask for consent to publish before they can be sent.

Share from your classroom (educators)

Asks for: your role and state; your school's type, setting and grades; which CS courses it offers; rough ranges for the share of girls, students of color and low-income students in CS classes (for example "under 25%"); barriers; whether anything changed since 2025; what has worked; and, if you choose, your name, school and an email.

Shown publicly: combined counts across all approved answers, never one person's answers. No counts are shown until at least 10 answers are approved. A quote may appear if the author picks one of your written answers, copied word for word, with your role and grades. Your state is shown only if at least 10 approved answers come from it, and your name and school only if you ticked the box to allow it.

Kept private: your email, your name and school unless you allowed them, and your full set of answers.

Outreach directory (program leaders)

Asks for: the program's name, organization, website, grades, who it serves, state and city, format, cost and scholarships, how teachers can connect students, whether funding or partnerships changed since 2025, and a contact name and email.

Shown publicly: the program listing: name, organization, website, grades, who it serves, place, format, cost, scholarships and how to connect students. The funding answer appears only inside a combined summary across all programs.

Kept private: the contact name and email, and any details you give about funding.

  • No student information. Neither form asks about any individual student. Educators give rough ranges for groups, never names or counts of students. If an answer mentions a student by name or in a way that could identify them, it is edited out or the answer is rejected.
  • Small numbers stay hidden. Any count under 10 is never shown, so a small school or program can't be picked out. When hiding one number would let you work it out from the total, a second number is hidden too.
  • Emails are never shown. Contact emails are used only to follow up and are never shown on the site or returned by any public part of it.
  • Spam protection without tracking. To limit repeated submissions, the site keeps a scrambled (hashed) version of your connection's address for one hour and then deletes it. The address itself is never stored.
