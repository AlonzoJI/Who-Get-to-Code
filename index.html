Who gets to be a computer scientist? The programs built to widen it are being rolled back.

A resource for high school computer science teachers and counselors. It follows students from the first CS class a school offers to their first job in tech, with cited and dated data at every step.

Where things stand

More schools offer the class. Who takes it hasn't caught up.

Access grew for years and has now leveled off. Students whose high school started offering computer science earned more by age 24, with a bigger gain for Black students, yet low-income students are still underrepresented in the room.

The argument

Access went up. Who sticks with it mostly didn't change.

More high schools offer computer science than ever before. The students who take it and stay with it still mostly look the same.

Since 2023, many of the programs built to widen that pipeline have been rolled back: in the courts, in federal agencies, in Ohio law and inside tech companies. This site follows outcomes, meaning who enrolls, who gets into which college and who gets hired, and asks what the rollback is doing to them.

Each policy section also lays out what supporters of the change argue, with a source, next to what the outcome data shows.

The technical version

What a point means. Each point is a group's share of the people counted at that stage, in percent: for example, Black students' share of AP CS exams taken. Each stage uses its own dataset. No stage is multiplied by another, and no number is carried from one stage to the next.

Index view. The index divides a group's share at a stage by its share of all public high school students. 1.0 is parity: the group is represented at that stage as it is in high school. Below 1.0 means underrepresented.

Comparability. Unknown race, nonresident and "no response" categories are left out and the remaining shares are re-totaled, so every stage adds up the same groups as the baseline. AP counts exams, so a student who took both AP CS exams counts twice. Stage 01 for race is derived from Code.org's access rates and the NCES baseline (hollow points). Stages describe different years and populations, so the chart compares snapshots, not one class followed through school and work.

Gaps. A stage with no verified figure for a group leaves a gap in that group's line and is marked "Placeholder data" in the stage list. Nothing is filled in.

The before/after toggle changes stage 04, which compares fall 2023 and fall 2024 first-year enrollment. Every other stage has one period.
