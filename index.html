Who gets to be a computer scientist? The programs built to widen it are being rolled back.

A resource for high school computer science teachers and counselors. It follows students from the first CS class a school offers to their first job in tech, with cited and dated data at every step.

Where things stand

More schools offer the class. Who takes it hasn't caught up.

Access grew for years and has now leveled off. Students whose high school started offering computer science earned more by age 24, with a bigger gain for Black students, yet low-income students are still underrepresented in the room.

The argument

Access went up. Who sticks with it mostly didn't change.

More high schools offer computer science than ever before. The students who take it and stay with it still mostly look the same.

Since 2023, many of the programs built to widen that pipeline have been rolled back: in the courts, in federal agencies, in Ohio law and inside tech companies. This site follows outcomes, meaning who enrolls, who gets into which college and who gets hired, and asks what the rollback is doing to them.

Each policy section also lays out what supporters of the change argue, with a source, next to what the outcome data shows.

The pipeline

Six steps, from a class on the schedule to a first job

Pick a group and press Run to follow 100 students from that group through every step. This is a projection built from published figures, not one class followed for ten years. The log on the side shows each rate, how it was estimated and where the numbers come from.

The interactive diagram needs JavaScript. The datasets for each stage are listed below.

Sources: NCES, CRDC, College Board, IPEDS, NCSES and Census figures, 2019 to 2024. Each step lists its own sources and years in the log.

The technical version

All students. Each step's rate is a ratio of published figures. Stage 01 is the share of high schoolers at a school offering CS (Code.org, 2021). Stage 02 is 2023–24 CRDC CS enrollment divided by fall 2023 grades 9–12 enrollment, adjusted for access. Stage 03 is 2024 AP CS Principles takers divided by CRDC enrollment. Stage 04 is College Board's estimate of four-year entry for students averaging a 3 on AP exams. Stage 05 multiplies the share of matched AP CSP takers who declared CS by the six-year bachelor's completion rate. Stage 06 is the share of CS bachelor's holders working as computer or mathematical scientists.

One group. The all-students rate is adjusted by how the group's share changes between two matching populations, such as its share of AP CSP takers compared with its share of CS enrollees. The adjustment scales the odds, so no rate goes above 100%. Where no breakdown exists (income at every stage, gender at stage 04), the step uses the all-students rate and the log says so.

What this is not. The figures come from different cohorts, years and sometimes different populations (some AP counts include private schools; the workforce figures cover all ages). The result shows the rough size of each drop-off and how groups compare. It is not a measured outcome. Dots are rounded to whole students, so the labels under each stage keep the decimals.

The before/after toggle changes stage 04 for race groups, using fall 2023 or fall 2024 first-year enrollment at four-year colleges.
