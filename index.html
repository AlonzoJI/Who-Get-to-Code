Who gets to be a computer scientist? The programs built to widen it are being rolled back.

A resource for high school computer science teachers and counselors. It follows students from the first CS class a school offers to their first job in tech, with cited and dated data at every step.

Where things stand

More schools offer the class. Who takes it hasn't caught up.

Access grew for years and has now leveled off. Students whose high school started offering computer science earned more by age 24, with a bigger gain for Black students, yet low-income students are still underrepresented in the room.

The argument

Access went up. Who sticks with it mostly didn't change.

More high schools offer computer science than ever before. The students who take it and stay with it still mostly look the same.

Since 2023, many of the programs built to widen that pipeline have been rolled back: in the courts, in federal agencies, in Ohio law and inside tech companies. This site follows outcomes, meaning who enrolls, who gets into which college and who gets hired, and asks what the rollback is doing to them.

Each policy section also lays out what supporters of the change argue, with a source, next to what the outcome data shows.

The pipeline

Six steps, from a class on the schedule to a first job

Pick a group and press Run to follow 100 students from that group through every step. The log on the side records where students drop off and which source each number comes from.

The interactive diagram needs JavaScript. The datasets for each stage are listed below.

The technical version

Each stage's number is the percent of the group who move on from the previous stage (stage 01 is out of all students in the group). The diagram multiplies those rates, so the count at stage 06 is the product of all six. Counts are rounded once per stage from the running product, so rounding never adds up across steps.

A stage with no verified number removes nobody and is marked "Placeholder data" in the log. Nothing is estimated or filled in. Datasets measure different cohorts and years, so the chained result describes a typical path, not one real class of students followed for ten years.

The before/after toggle only changes stages that have a verified figure for both periods. Every other stage uses the same figure either way.
